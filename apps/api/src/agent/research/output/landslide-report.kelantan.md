# Comprehensive Analysis of Rainfall-Induced Landslide Thresholds in Peninsular Malaysia and the Kelantan River Basin

## Executive Summary
This report provides an exhaustive, expert-level analysis of rainfall-induced landslide thresholds across Peninsular Malaysia, with a specific focus on their applicability to the Kelantan River Valley. Humid tropical regions such as Malaysia present unique geomorphological and geotechnical challenges due to deep residual soil profiles, aggressive chemical weathering, and extreme monsoon precipitation. The analysis reveals that Malaysian slope governance, directed primarily by the Public Works Department (JKR) and the Minerals and Geoscience Department (JMG), officially treats slopes exceeding a 25-degree angle as landslide-prone. These are administratively classified as "Class 3" terrains, requiring strict environmental impact assessments and geotechnical vulnerability reports [cite: 1, 2, 3]. 

Empirical cumulative rainfall thresholds indicate that shallow slope failures in the region are closely associated with baseline accumulations of 21.04 mm over one day, 47.66 mm over three days, and 67.09 mm over five days [cite: 4]. However, the operational alert, warning, and danger levels utilized by multi-agency early warning systems scale significantly higher to account for regional hydro-geological resilience. "Danger" levels are designated at accumulations exceeding 100 mm per day under historical JKR guidelines [cite: 5] and escalate to exceeding 240 mm per day for meteorological disaster warnings issued by MetMalaysia [cite: 6]. The historical December 2014 "Bah Kuning" floods in Kelantan entirely obliterated these standard predictive thresholds, recording catastrophic extreme events such as 1,295 mm of rainfall over three days in Gua Musang [cite: 7, 8]. Following this summary is a synthesized table of thresholds and intensity-duration (I-D) equations, which precedes an extensive technical critique regarding the spatial transposition of West Coast empirical models to the climatologically distinct East Coast state of Kelantan.

## Caveats on Uncertainty and Applicability to the Kelantan Valley

Applying generic Peninsular Malaysian empirical rainfall thresholds directly to the Kelantan River Valley demands rigorous scientific caution. While exhaustive studies exist for the broader nation, there is an acute spatial bias in the historical literature. The overwhelming majority of detailed intensity-duration threshold parameters, early warning pilot projects, and soil matric suction studies have been disproportionately concentrated on the West Coast, specifically in densely populated areas like Hulu Kelang, Ampang, Penang, and the central Cameron Highlands [cite: 10, 13, 14, 15]. Directly transposing these West Coast thresholds to the East Coast state of Kelantan introduces a high probability of systemic false alarms due to the climatological asymmetry of the Malaysian peninsula.

The East Coast bears the brunt of the Northeast Monsoon from November to March, during which it is subjected to vastly higher routine cumulative rainfall [cite: 16, 17]. Slopes in the Kelantan River Basin have evolved hydro-geomorphologically under these wetter conditions, developing deeper drainage capacities. Consequently, a three-day cumulative rainfall of 47.66 mm—which represents a 50% landslide probability alert on a generalized national scale [cite: 4]—is a frequent and entirely benign occurrence in Kelantan during the monsoon season. Utilizing a standard mean equation, such as $I = 16.1 D^{-0.722}$ [cite: 10], in an uncalibrated Kelantan early warning system would likely trigger continuous, erroneous evacuation orders, eventually leading to severe public desensitization to disaster warnings.

The most geographically relevant threshold analysis was recently conducted by Khalid (2022), who investigated the effect of rainfall on slope displacement affecting transmission towers situated directly on the hilly topography at the border of the Cameron Highlands and Kelantan [cite: 18]. This research fundamentally confirmed that five-day antecedent cumulative rainfall is the paramount variable for predicting landslide initiation in this specific East Coast transitional terrain [cite: 18]. Through advanced statistical modeling, the study demonstrated that 91% of predictive rainfall events closely correlated with physical slope displacement data in this border region [cite: 18]. This reinforces the geotechnical premise that while the general physics of infiltration and pore water pressure hold true across the peninsula, the specific volumetric accumulation limits must be aggressively stretched in Kelantan to account for local soil drainage capabilities, deeper groundwater table fluctuations, and the extreme baseline set by the Northeast Monsoon.

Furthermore, empirical threshold uncertainty is exacerbated by the physical distance between rain gauges and landslide initiation points. Many studies in Malaysia are forced to construct their I-D curves utilizing data from historical rain gauges located five to twenty kilometers away from the actual slope failure [cite: 10, 18]. In humid tropical regions characterized by highly localized, intense convective thunderstorms, a rain gauge ten kilometers away may record a moderate 20 mm event, while the actual slope experiences an extreme 80 mm deluge. This discrepancy artificially skews the derived threshold equations, leading to overly conservative models. Modern risk mitigation efforts by the Slope Engineering Branch are attempting to circumvent this systemic error by leveraging Next-Generation radar, telemetric systems, and machine-learning frameworks to capture high-resolution spatial variability [cite: 17, 19, 20, 21]. Until these hyper-localized models are fully operationalized in the Kelantan basin, relying solely on generic national thresholds remains highly problematic.

## The Geomorphological and Climatological Context of Malaysian Landslides

Peninsular Malaysia is characterized by a humid tropical climate, receiving an average annual rainfall that fluctuates between 2,000 mm and 4,000 mm depending on regional topography [cite: 22, 23, 24]. This climatic reality, featuring consistently high ambient temperatures and intense atmospheric moisture, accelerates the profound chemical weathering of underlying bedrock. This is particularly evident in the pervasive granitic and metasedimentary formations found across the peninsula's mountainous spine [cite: 24]. The culmination of this geological process is a deep, highly permeable residual soil profile that can extend tens of meters below the surface, creating unique vulnerabilities [cite: 15].

The geotechnical mechanics of rainfall-induced landslides in these tropical residual soils are fundamentally tied to the delicate balance between transient pore water pressure and matric suction. During extended dry periods, negative pore water pressure, known as matric suction, acts as a binding agent, providing apparent cohesion that stabilizes naturally steep slopes and artificial cut slopes. However, during periods of prolonged or intense monsoon precipitation, continuous rainwater infiltration gradually diminishes this matric suction [cite: 15, 25]. As the soil profile approaches total saturation, the apparent cohesion collapses, rapidly decreasing the soil's shear strength and driving the slope's overall factor of safety below equilibrium [cite: 15, 25]. 

Consequently, empirical rainfall thresholds in Malaysia frequently dominate the "upper positions" when compared to global empirical thresholds, such as the widely cited Caine threshold. The immense thickness and initially high permeability of Malaysian residual soils demand substantially higher volumes of continuous infiltration to achieve the critical saturated state required for massive slope failure [cite: 10]. This explains why short, intense bursts of rain are less likely to cause deep-seated failures compared to weeks of moderate, antecedent rainfall that methodically raises the local groundwater table.

### Institutional Frameworks and the Evolution of the National Slope Master Plan

Following a series of catastrophic slope failures that exacted severe economic and human costs—most notably the 1993 Highland Towers collapse, the 2003 Bukit Lanjan rockfall, and the devastating 2008 Bukit Antarabangsa tragedy—the Malaysian government recognized that landslides were no longer merely isolated engineering failures, but a national socio-economic crisis [cite: 26, 27]. Economic losses attributed to landslides in Malaysia since 1973 are estimated to exceed one billion US dollars [cite: 1, 13]. In response to the Bukit Lanjan incident, which paralyzed a major arterial highway and caused nearly three-quarters of a billion ringgit in economic disruption, the Malaysian Cabinet mandated a comprehensive regulatory overhaul [cite: 26]. 

This resulted in the establishment of the Slope Engineering Branch, known natively as Cawangan Kejuruteraan Cerun (CKC), under the Public Works Department (JKR) in 2004 [cite: 26, 28]. The CKC subsequently commissioned and developed the National Slope Master Plan (NSMP) 2009-2023, a seminal 15-year strategic roadmap targeting landslide risk reduction [cite: 26, 28]. The NSMP is structured around ten core components, prioritizing hazard mapping and assessment, the development of early warning systems, real-time monitoring, loss assessment, and policy institutionalization [cite: 26, 28]. 

The implementation of Territorial Landslide Early Warning Systems (Te-LEWS) under the NSMP involves complex, multi-agency coordination. The Department of Mineral and Geoscience (JMG) operates as the primary repository for lithological data, conducting area-based landslide hazard and risk mapping across critical zones such as the Klang Valley, Cameron Highlands, and parts of Sabah [cite: 16, 26]. Simultaneously, MetMalaysia supplies the critical meteorological architecture, issuing precipitation forecasts and continuous heavy rain alerts [cite: 6, 26]. These agencies work in tandem with the CKC, which correlates the meteorological indices with actual ground movement, utilizing networks of telemetric rain gauges, robotic total stations, and Global Navigation Satellite Systems (GNSS) to monitor the physical precursors to slope failure [cite: 21]. Furthermore, the CKC has been aggressively transitioning from traditional deterministic safety factors to Quantitative Risk Assessment (QRA) frameworks, utilizing F-N (Frequency-Fatality) charts to establish societal risk criteria and prioritize the rectification of existing high-risk slopes [cite: 29].

## Regulatory Frameworks and Minimum Slope Angle Thresholds

In Malaysia, the regulatory definition of a "landslide-prone" slope is institutionalized through the Ministry of Housing and Local Government (KPKT) guidelines on hilly terrain physical development. These guidelines form the absolute foundation for JKR assessments, municipal planning approvals, and infrastructure risk analyses [cite: 1, 2, 3]. The regulatory framework categorizes slopes into four distinct classes based solely on their gradient, directly correlating the slope angle with escalating administrative, environmental, and engineering restrictions. 

Class 1 slopes are those with a gradient below 15 degrees. These are considered low-risk environments where standard development is permitted, provided there is strict compliance with local government erosion guidelines and environmentally friendly drainage manuals [cite: 1, 2]. Class 2 slopes range from 15 to 25 degrees and represent a medium risk. For developments in this class that exceed 50 hectares, a formal Environmental Impact Assessment (EIA) is mandated [cite: 1, 2].

The critical regulatory threshold is crossed at Class 3, which encompasses slopes with a gradient between 25 and 35 degrees. Any slope exceeding a 25-degree angle is officially treated by Malaysian authorities as a highly landslide-prone hazard [cite: 1, 2, 27]. Development within this 25-degree threshold is subjected to severe restrictions. It requires a mandatory, detailed Environmental Impact Assessment (EIA) regardless of the project's overall size, alongside a comprehensive Landslide Vulnerability Assessment (LVA) and extensive geotechnical stability reports [cite: 1, 2, 27]. These reports must detail erosion risk maps and explicit stabilization proposals to mitigate the inherent dangers of altering the terrain [cite: 27]. 

Class 4 encompasses extreme-risk slopes with a gradient above 35 degrees. In these zones, physical development projects are strictly prohibited by law, with the singular exception of vital infrastructure such as unavoidable road construction. Even in those inevitable cases, exhaustive LVA and EIA processes are required to authorize the engineering works [cite: 1, 2]. 

Therefore, the minimum slope angle above which terrains are legally, structurally, and administratively treated as landslide-prone hazards requiring specialized mitigation in Malaysian guidance is definitively 25 degrees. This 25-degree threshold is echoed by the Institution of Engineers Malaysia (IEM), which explicitly classifies natural or man-made slopes of this angle as demanding high-tier geotechnical interventions and independent peer reviews during the design phase [cite: 30].

## Empirical Rainfall Thresholds and Intensity-Duration (I-D) Models

Developing reliable rainfall thresholds for landslide initiation is an inherently complex endeavor that must bridge atmospheric science and soil mechanics. Empirical models in Malaysia primarily focus on either the interaction between antecedent cumulative rainfall and acute event rainfall, or the power-law relationship between rainfall intensity and storm duration.

### Cumulative and Antecedent Rainfall Drivers
Research spanning Peninsular Malaysia consistently indicates that shallow landslides are seldom triggered by brief, isolated downpours; rather, they are the culmination of intense antecedent priming. Studies reveal that accumulated rainfall over continuous periods of 1 to 15 days is the critical factor required to overcome the residual soil's permeability threshold, allowing the wetting front to advance deeply enough to destroy stabilizing matric suction [cite: 10, 18]. 

A comprehensive nationwide assessment established generic, critical cumulative precipitation amounts required to reach a 50% probability of a landslide initiation event. These thresholds are defined as 21.04 mm over a single day, 35.74 mm over two days, 47.66 mm over three days, 57.94 mm over four days, and 67.09 mm over five days [cite: 4]. The same study observed a broader trend across the ASEAN tropical belt, noting that when accumulated rainfall surpasses 250 mm in the 15 days immediately prior to an event, the probability of failure spikes violently, accounting for over 30% of total landslides in the region [cite: 4]. 

These findings are corroborated by localized studies. Advanced machine learning assessments conducted on the West Coast, specifically analyzing Federal Route FT185 between Simpang Pulai and Blue Valley, demonstrated that 3-day and 5-day antecedent cumulative rainfall windows were significantly superior predictors of shallow slope failure when compared to isolated single-day metrics [cite: 20]. Similarly, in the highly susceptible Hulu Kelang area, a specialized E3-API30 model was developed to integrate 3-day cumulative rainfall with a 30-day antecedent precipitation index. This model achieved a 97.6% reliability rate by highlighting that long-term moisture accumulation is an inescapable prerequisite for major slope failures in deep granitic residual soils [cite: 15, 31].

### Intensity-Duration (I-D) Equations
To standardize early warnings, several specific Intensity-Duration (I-D) equations have been proposed for Peninsular Malaysia. These primarily take the standard mathematical power-law form $I = \alpha D^{-\beta}$, where $I$ is the rainfall intensity and $D$ is the duration.

The most widely validated recent models establish a revised mean boundary for shallow landslide initiation across Peninsular Malaysia for event durations between 1 and 263 hours, calculated as $I = 16.1 D^{-0.722}$. This equation was explicitly adjusted down from an initial parameter of 17.5 to account for false-negative events during validation against actual historical slides [cite: 10]. The corresponding maximum threshold, representing a high-credibility upper-bound critical intensity limit, is defined as $I = 37.8 D^{-0.114}$ [cite: 10]. 

Regional variations exist to account for specific microclimates and altitudes. For the Cameron Highlands, a highly weathered agricultural and tourist region characterized by frequent mudflows and shallow slides, the regional I-D threshold is calculated at $I = 29.088 D^{-0.075}$ [cite: 11, 12, 14]. The extremely shallow negative exponent (-0.075) in the Cameron Highlands equation is highly significant; it mathematically demonstrates that the required rainfall intensity to trigger a failure decays very slowly even as the storm duration extends. This indicates a terrain so saturated and geologically fragile that even a marginal increase in prolonged, low-intensity rain can initiate a catastrophic slope failure. Conversely, an older baseline minimum equation utilized for the wider peninsula to determine the absolute minimum average rainfall capable of triggering shallow landslides was calculated as $I = 14.52 D^{-0.473}$ [cite: 4]. 

## Operational Alert, Warning, and Danger Levels

Translating these empirical models into operational early warning systems requires discrete, actionable categorization. Because low-level cumulative thresholds (e.g., 67 mm over 5 days) are breached routinely during the monsoon without resulting in failure, Malaysian disaster agencies utilize varying tiered systems that scale much higher to prevent alert fatigue.

MetMalaysia manages the national meteorological alerting architecture. Their system issues alerts based on the expectation of continuous precipitation. An "Alert" level signifies the forecast of continuous rain; a "Severe" warning denotes continuous heavy rain; and the ultimate "Danger" level is triggered when continuous heavy rain is expected to exceed a cumulative total of 240 mm per day [cite: 6]. This exceptionally high threshold reflects the atmospheric conditions necessary to cause widespread, regional systemic failures rather than isolated single-slope collapses.

From a geotechnical engineering perspective, the Public Works Department (JKR) utilizes criteria heavily influenced by collaborative studies with the Japan International Cooperation Agency (JICA). Historical guidelines established for Federal Roads define four operational alert levels for slope monitoring, tracking both physical displacement (via extensometers and inclinometers) and rainfall. Level 1 requires site inspection and frequent monitoring; Level 2 initiates committee mobilization; Level 3 mandates preliminary alerts and emergency measures; and Level 4 represents the "Danger Level," demanding immediate evacuation and road closure. The explicit rainfall threshold to trigger a Level 4 Danger alert in these guidelines is set at continuous rainfall exceeding 100 mm per day [cite: 5]. 

To operationalize these thresholds, the JKR Slope Engineering Branch utilizes an advanced Landslide Early Warning System (EWS) known as BiGBen. This system relies on a network of over 70 telemetric rain gauge stations installed across Malaysia [cite: 21]. The system continuously ingests rainfall index data; when local thresholds are met, the telemetry systems issue automated warnings, triggering operational measures such as pre-identified alternative routes, staged road closures, and traffic diversions coordinated with emergency services [cite: 21].

## The December 2014 "Bah Kuning" Event: Kelantan's Extreme Baseline

To genuinely understand landslide and flood triggers in the Kelantan River Basin, predictive models must be weighed against the catastrophic December 2014 monsoon event. Locally termed "Bah Kuning" (the Yellow Flood) due to the immense volume of suspended sediment, mudflow, and uprooted timber stripped from upstream hillsides, this event represents the extreme hydrological baseline for the East Coast [cite: 7, 8].

Between December 15, 2014, and January 1, 2015, the East Coast of Peninsular Malaysia was subjected to anomalous, relentless precipitation [cite: 8, 9]. This was not a standard seasonal monsoon; it was a rare meteorological convergence driven by the Northeast Monsoon interacting simultaneously with the Madden-Julian Oscillation (MJO) and a high-pressure cold surge descending from the Siberian High [cite: 9, 32]. The spatial accumulation patterns resulted in staggering rainfall depths that obliterated all known predictive landslide and flood thresholds for the region. 

In Gua Musang, located in the mountainous southern interior of Kelantan, an astonishing 1,295 mm of rain fell in just three days between December 21 and December 23. This represents a concentrated accumulation typically expected to fall over a span of 64 days [cite: 7, 8]. Further upstream, the Gunung Gagau station recorded an equally devastating 7-day cumulative rainfall of 1,598.9 mm, compared to its historical 7-day average of just 976.5 mm [cite: 9]. Upstream river stations experienced Average Recurrence Intervals (ARIs) exceeding 100 to 200 years, with the estimated return period of the overall regional rainfall event reaching an unprecedented 1:1000 years [cite: 8, 9]. 

The resulting geological trauma was immense. The rapid, colossal saturation of upland terrain—which had already been severely compromised by aggressive decades of deforestation and the conversion of primary forests to oil palm and mixed agriculture—resulted in massive, instantaneous soil erosion and widespread debris flows [cite: 8]. Deeply weathered soils completely liquefied, causing cascading slope failures that discharged vast quantities of sand, clay, and timber downslope. This choked river networks, silted floodplains, forced the displacement of over 200,000 residents, and severely crippled critical infrastructure, including the near-total operational collapse of hospitals in Kuala Krai [cite: 7, 8, 31]. 

This 2014 event provides a critical insight into regional disaster management: In extreme monsoon states, empirical I-D equations become functionally obsolete. When daily rainfall routinely surpasses 300 mm to 500 mm over consecutive days across an entire catchment [cite: 9], the scientific question of *if* a landslide will occur shifts entirely to *where* the topography is most susceptible. The soil saturation threshold is uniformly breached across the entire basin, shifting disaster management strictly from predictive geotechnical modeling to emergency evacuation protocols. Future modeling for the Kelantan valley must inherently acknowledge that when the Northeast monsoon aligns with anomalous oceanic oscillations, standard thresholds of 67 mm or even 100 mm are rendered irrelevant in the face of precipitation events exceeding 1,000 mm.
